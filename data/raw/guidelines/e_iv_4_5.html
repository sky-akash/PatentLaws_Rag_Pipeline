4.5 Evaluation of the testimony of a witness 

After the witnesses have been heard, the party or parties must be given an opportunity to make observations. The observations may be made either at oral proceedings after evidence has been taken or, exceptionally, in writing after minutes of the taking of evidence have been transmitted. The competent department will decide on this. The parties may file requests accordingly.

Only when this has been done may the competent department proceed to evaluate the evidence. Where a witness's testimony which is crucial to the decision has been challenged by a party but the department regards it as credible, or where it disregards a witness's oral or written testimony in its decision as not credible, it must state the grounds for its view in its decision.

In evaluating a witness's oral or written testimony, special attention is to be paid to the following:

(i)What is important is what witnesses can relate on the points at issue on the basis of their own knowledge or views, and whether they have practical experience in the field in question. Second-hand assertions based on something heard from third parties are for the most part worthless on their own. It is also important for the purposes of the evaluation whether the witness was involved in the event or only knows of it as an observer or listener. 

(ii)Where a long time (several years) has passed between the event in question and the testimony, it is to be borne in mind that most people's powers of recollection are limited without the support of documentary evidence. 

(iii)Where testimony appears to conflict, the texts of the statements concerned are to be closely compared with one another. 

Apparent contradictions in the testimony of witnesses may sometimes be resolved in this way. For example, a close examination of apparently contradictory statements by witnesses as to whether a substance X was commonly used for a particular purpose may show that there is in fact no contradiction at all, in that while one witness was saying specifically that substance X was not used for that particular purpose, the other witness was saying no more than that substances like X, or a certain class of substances to which X belonged, were commonly used for this particular purpose, without intending to make any statement about substance X itself.

(iv)An employee of a party to the proceedings can be heard as a witness (see T 482/89). The possible partiality of a witness determines how the evidence is assessed, not whether it is admissible (see T 443/93).
